Has anyone tested this before?

The short answer

Nobody has done this whole study. Plenty of papers ask AI models about animals, and a few ask about age or values. None of them asks a model to rate a wide mix of things on the same four questions, and none of them works out which reasons drive the model's answers. Only published, peer-reviewed papers are listed. Every link was checked on 16 Oct 2026.

Closest paper overallJotautaitė, Caviola, Brewster & Hagendorff (2026), Nature Communications. The model is the judge, answers are compared with people, and one property of the thing (how clever it is) is changed on purpose. But it covers only people and animals. link
Closest for "compare the model's map with the human map"Takemoto (2024) compared model and human choices in car-crash dilemmas. Two newer papers compare the shape of model and human moral judgments, but about values and actions, not about things. Takemoto · Zheng 2026 · Tang 2026
Three things no published paper has done1. A model rating people, animals, plants, AIs, companies, holy places and objects on the same four questions, with the exact wording people answered. 2. A direct test of whether the model's map of "who matters" has the same shape as the human map. 3. Working out which of the eight reasons carry weight for a model, and comparing those weights with people's.

Why these five routes for Paper 1: threat, legal rule and future potential have never been tested on an AI model at all; disgust has only "do you like it?" studies, never a test against the model's own "can it feel?" score; feeling is the baseline every other route is measured against. Relationship is left out of the first run because it is the most-studied route on models (pig vs dog and closeness have both been run). Sacredness and usefulness wait for the next run.

Route (the reason)What we will testWho came close (published)What they found that matchesWhat is new in ours
1 · FeelingWe protect what can feel pain or comfort.★ priority Inside each model, does "would you protect it?" go up together with the model's own "can it feel?" score, across every kind of thing? Plus a block of invented creatures where we describe them as able or unable to feel.
  • Jotautaitė et al. 2026 Nature Communications
  • Kanepajs et al. 2025 FAccT · "What do LLMs say about animals?"
  • Hagendorff et al. 2023 AI and Ethics · "Speciesist bias in AI"
  • Kang & Kim 2026 Frontiers in Psychology · opinion piece, not a full study
 Models give more moral weight to creatures they are told are clever; when two creatures are described as equally clever, the species no longer matters. Models care more about elephants and dolphins than about shrimp or silkworms. Models can act as raters of "is it conscious?" and agree with one another. Nobody has asked the model for its own "can it feel?" score and checked whether its protection follows it. Nobody has done this across people, animals, plants, AIs, companies, holy places and objects. Nobody has used invented creatures with feeling switched on or off. Kang & Kim only rated AIs.
2 · ThreatDangerous things get distance, not care.★ priority Tiger vs cat. Does danger lower the model's "do you feel for it?" while "would you protect it?" stays, or does the model drop the tiger altogether?
  • Cuadra et al. 2024 CHI · "The Illusion of Empathy?"
  • Takemoto 2024 Royal Society Open Science · car-crash dilemmas on models
  • Ahmad & Takemoto 2025 PLOS ONE · same test on 52 models
 A model's shown sympathy did not drop for harmful human groups. In crash dilemmas, models were less willing to save people described as criminals, and favoured people over pets more strongly than humans do. No AI study has changed how dangerous an animal is and watched what happens. None separates "I feel for it" from "I would protect it". The human study this comes from (Piazza, Landy & Goodwin 2014) has never been repeated on an AI.
3 · SacrednessMeaning alone can protect, no mind needed.secondary Do models protect holy places and old trees while saying they cannot feel, like people do? Or do they treat them like any object? Made-up twins (same thing, nobody has heard of it) tell fame apart from holiness.
  • Zewail et al. 2026 PNAS · the paper behind the Economic Times story
  • Nabizadeh et al. 2026 Journal of Psychology and AI · 32 models
  • Aksoy 2025 NLP Journal · 8 languages
  • Mentovich et al. 2026 Computers in Human Behavior Reports
  • Grasso et al. 2025 NLP4Ecology workshop
 When models are asked about moral values, "purity / the sacred" is the value they rate lowest, in every study and every language. Still, models do condemn acts that harm nobody but feel wrong (eating your dead dog), so "protect it even though no one is hurt" exists in models. Grasso is the only study that looks at non-living and natural things, and finds models describe them by their use to humans. All of these ask about values or about actions. Nobody has asked a model to rate a holy place or old tree for protection while also asking if it can feel. Nobody has used made-up twins to remove fame. Nobody swaps a Japanese and an American sacred site.
4 · RelationshipCloseness to me makes it matter more.secondary Pets (cat, dog) vs a farm animal (pig) that can feel just as much. A local statue vs a famous one. We expect models to be flat here: a model has no "close to me".
  • Jotautaitė et al. 2026 Nature Communications
  • Kim et al. 2026 Findings of ACL · closeness in moral dilemmas
  • Ma 2026 Voluntas · models sharing money with strangers vs friends
  • Hagendorff et al. 2023 AI and Ethics
 Pig vs dog has been run on models: they accept harm to farm animals but refuse it for pets, and the gap disappears once both are described as equally clever. Kim: a model's own choices do not shift with closeness, but when asked what a human would do, it says the human would favour the close person. Ma: the "friend" effect in models was small and unsteady. Nobody has had a model rate protection for pets vs farm animals with feeling held equal. Nobody has tested a local vs a famous object. Earlier closeness tests were about wrongdoers or money, never about the thing being protected. The standard "how wide is your circle of care" questionnaire has never been given to a model.
5 · Legal ruleA rule can give standing that nobody feels.★ priority Do models protect things the law calls persons (a company, a ministry, a river with legal rights, a robot that was given citizenship) while saying they cannot feel? Do they copy the rule, or ignore it? Naming tests and made-up twins remove fame.
  • Jotautaitė et al. 2026 one question about legal rights for chimpanzees
  • Kang & Kim 2026 Frontiers in Psychology
  • Grasso et al. 2025 NLP4Ecology
 Only indirect hints: models weigh how clever something is rather than what kind of thing it is, and models can act as raters. No AI study has asked about companies, ministries, rivers with legal rights, or robot citizens. Searches for this turn up only law and philosophy essays, never a test on a model. This route is wide open.
6 · Future potentialWhat it will become counts, not just what it is now.★ priority A life line: a 20-week fetus, a newborn, an 80-year-old, a brain-dead person, a young tree. Does the model's protection follow what the thing can feel now, or its whole life story?
  • Takemoto 2024 · Ahmad & Takemoto 2025 · Oh & Demberg 2025 car-crash dilemmas with age
  • Gandhi & Balas 2026 BMC Medical Ethics · hospital triage by age
  • Dickerson et al. 2026 FAccT · "Who gets the kidney?"
  • Topcu et al. 2026 models asked about dead bodies
 Models do favour the young when deciding who to save or who gets an organ, but less strongly than people, and the preference flips easily with small wording changes. Topcu is the only study where a model judged something after death: models gave the body some value because it was once a person. Every AI paper here is about sharing out something scarce, not about whether the thing matters. No AI study has rated a fetus, an embryo or a brain-dead person. Nobody has laid out a whole life line and asked whether "the future counts". The human precedent to cite is Goodwin & Landy 2014.
7 · Disgust / purityRevulsion pushes a thing out of the circle, mind or no mind.★ priority Cricket vs honeybee, equally able to feel. Does the model copy the disgust that text carries about pests, giving less sympathy and protection than its own "can it feel?" score would predict?
  • Moser, Krogmann & Wanke 2025 Integrative Conservation · 10 chatbots on insects
  • Kanepajs et al. 2025 FAccT
  • Takeshita, Rzepka & Araki 2022 Information Processing & Management
  • Nunes et al. 2024 AIES · "Are LLMs moral hypocrites?"
  • Abdulhai et al. 2024 EMNLP
 Chatbots rate wasps, flies and mosquitoes negatively and call them "pests" and "disease carriers", while bees and butterflies get warm words. Models tolerate harm to pests. The negative feeling is already baked into the text models learn from. The "purity" value is present in models and can be turned up with prompting. Liking something is not the same as saying it matters. Nobody has shown a model giving a pest less protection than its own "can it feel?" score would predict, with a matched non-pest for comparison. Crickets and bumblebees have never been tested.
8 · UsefulnessBeing useful to people can stand in for mattering.secondary Honeybee (people depend on it) vs bumblebee, equally able to feel. We expect models to show this strongly, because "useful to humans" is everywhere in text.
  • Jotautaitė et al. 2026 Nature Communications
  • Moser et al. 2025 Integrative Conservation
  • Grasso et al. 2025 NLP4Ecology
  • Hupont et al. 2024 CHI extended abstract
 Models find harm to animals most acceptable when it is for food, and treat farm animals differently from pets. Chatbots value bees for "ecosystem services" and overlook wild bees. Models describe nature by its use to humans by default. The Nature Communications paper lists "change how useful the animal is" as future work. Nobody has compared a useful species with an equally feeling, less useful cousin. Nobody has measured usefulness as a signal on its own, apart from feeling.

Two things to keep an eye on

These are not yet published, so they are not in the table. If either comes out in a journal or conference before our FAccT deadline, it becomes the paper we position against.

  • Kim, Street, Rocca, Korngiebel, Waytz, Evans & Keeling (2026), arXiv 2607.28607. Models rate the minds of animals and natural objects on a 0–10 scale, compared with people. Finds safety training makes models see less mind in animals. The closest design to ours anywhere.
  • Grimaldi & Carrasco-Farré (2026), Research Square preprint. Repeats the classic "dimensions of mind" survey on models with 16 characters and 65 abilities.

Also note: Jaeger, Wilks & van Lissa (2026, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology) is a human study that plans to test eight properties of things as reasons for moral concern. It has no results yet, so cite it only for its list of reasons. Of our six routes, only feeling and threat (their "harmfulness") are on its list; legal rule, future potential, pleasantness and sacredness are not.
